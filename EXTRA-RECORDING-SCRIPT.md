# Extra recordings: The Code of Hammurabi

Two kinds of short clips that go with the reading clips you already have (or will have). Use the **same voice and settings** as the readings so it all sounds like one narrator. Send the MP3s to Claude, who will match the volume, name them and place them.

## Part 1: "Look closely" prompts

These play **before** the reading, when a student opens a dot and sees the *Look closely* prompt. They add a **Listen** button to the prompt. Each is about 10–15 seconds. Leave a short pause after the question.

Files: `exhibits/hammurabi/audio/en/01-notice.mp3` … `09-notice.mp3`, and the same names in `audio/es/`.

Every prompt ends with the same sentence starters. Read them each time, or record them once and Claude will add them to each clip.

### English

#### `01-notice.mp3` (dot 1: Source detective)

> Look closely.
>
> Two people are carved at the top. One is standing and one is sitting. What do you notice about each one? Who might they be?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `02-notice.mp3` (dot 2: Rivers, farms and rules)

> Look closely.
>
> You can't read this writing yet, but people long ago could. Many of these laws are about something farmers needed every day. What do you think it was?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `03-notice.mp3` (dot 3: Read the writing)

> Look closely.
>
> Get close and look at the writing. What shapes do you see? How do you think someone made these marks?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `04-notice.mp3` (dot 4: The missing laws)

> Look closely.
>
> Look near the bottom of this side. What is different about this part of the stone? What could have happened here?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `05-notice.mp3` (dot 5: Then and now)

> Look closely.
>
> This huge stone was found far away from the city where it was made. How do you think it got there?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `06-notice.mp3` (dot 6: Fair for everyone?)

> Look closely.
>
> The laws on this side are about punishments. Do you think everyone got the same punishment? Make a guess before you read.
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `07-notice.mp3` (dot 7: Rules for builders)

> Look closely.
>
> This part is about builders. If a house fell down, who should be in trouble? Guess what Hammurabi decided.
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `08-notice.mp3` (dot 8: How did people know the laws?)

> Look closely.
>
> Look how big this stone is. Why would a king put his laws on a giant stone where people could see it?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

#### `09-notice.mp3` (dot 9: How tall is it?)

> Look closely.
>
> Stand next to the stone. Guess: how tall is it? Is it taller than your teacher?
>
> Talk about it with the people near you. You can start with: I notice… I think it might be… because…

## Part 2: "Tell me more"

These play when a student opens **Tell me more** and presses **Listen to more**. Only 6 dots have a Tell me more section. Each clip reads the extra fact, then the original words (if the dot has them), and runs about 15–30 seconds. Read the original words a little more slowly; they're old-fashioned on purpose.

Files: `exhibits/hammurabi/audio/en/NN-more.mp3` (the dot numbers below), and the same names in `audio/es/`.

The Spanish original words are new Spanish translations of King's 1915 English, written for this exhibit. Please have your Spanish speaker check them along with the rest of the Spanish.

### English

#### `02-more.mp3` (dot 2: Rivers, farms and rules)

> Another law helped farmers: if a storm or a drought ruined a farmer's crops, he did not have to pay his debts that year (Law 48).
>
> Here are the original words, from Law 53, in a translation from 1915:
>
> If any one be too lazy to keep his dam in proper condition, and does not so keep it; if then the dam break and all the fields be flooded, then shall he in whose dam the break occurred be sold for money, and the money shall replace the corn which he has caused to be ruined.

#### `04-more.mp3` (dot 4: The missing laws)

> Archaeologists from France found the stone in Susa in 1901 and 1902, broken into pieces. Today it is in the Louvre museum in Paris. Some of the missing laws were found on clay copies.

#### `06-more.mp3` (dot 6: Fair for everyone?)

> Here are the original words, from Laws 196, 198, 199, in a translation from 1915:
>
> If a man put out the eye of another man, his eye shall be put out. … If he put out the eye of a freed man… he shall pay one gold mina. If he put out the eye of a man's slave… he shall pay one-half of its value.

#### `07-more.mp3` (dot 7: Rules for builders)

> Here are the original words, from Laws 229 and 233, in a translation from 1915:
>
> If a builder build a house for some one, and does not construct it properly, and the house which he built fall in and kill its owner, then that builder shall be put to death.

#### `08-more.mp3` (dot 8: How did people know the laws?)

> Here are the original words, from the ending (epilogue), in a translation from 1915:
>
> Let the oppressed, who has a case at law, come and stand before this my image as king of righteousness; let him read the inscription, and understand my precious words: the inscription will explain his case to him.

#### `09-more.mp3` (dot 9: How tall is it?)

> This 3D model is a scan of a plaster copy at Harvard's Museum of the Ancient Near East. The real stone is in the Louvre museum in Paris, France.

### Español

#### `02-more.mp3` (dot 2: Ríos, granjas y reglas)

> Otra ley ayudaba a los agricultores: si una tormenta o una sequía arruinaba su cosecha, no tenían que pagar sus deudas ese año (Ley 48).
>
> Estas son las palabras originales, de la Ley 53, en una traducción antigua:
>
> Si alguien es demasiado perezoso para mantener su dique en buen estado, y no lo mantiene así; si entonces el dique se rompe y todos los campos se inundan, aquel en cuyo dique ocurrió la rotura será vendido por dinero, y el dinero repondrá el grano que hizo que se perdiera.

#### `04-more.mp3` (dot 4: Las leyes que faltan)

> Arqueólogos de Francia encontraron la piedra en Susa en 1901 y 1902, rota en pedazos. Hoy está en el museo del Louvre, en París. Algunas de las leyes que faltaban se encontraron en copias de barro.

#### `06-more.mp3` (dot 6: ¿Justo para todos?)

> Estas son las palabras originales, de las Leyes 196, 198 y 199, en una traducción antigua:
>
> Si un hombre le saca el ojo a otro hombre, que le saquen el ojo a él. … Si le saca el ojo a un hombre liberto… pagará una mina de oro. Si le saca el ojo al esclavo de un hombre… pagará la mitad de su valor.

#### `07-more.mp3` (dot 7: Reglas para constructores)

> Estas son las palabras originales, de las Leyes 229 y 233, en una traducción antigua:
>
> Si un constructor construye una casa para alguien, y no la construye como es debido, y la casa que construyó se derrumba y mata a su dueño, entonces ese constructor será condenado a muerte.

#### `08-more.mp3` (dot 8: ¿Cómo conocía la gente las leyes?)

> Estas son las palabras originales, del final (epílogo), en una traducción antigua:
>
> Que el oprimido que tenga un caso ante la ley venga y se pare frente a esta imagen mía, como rey de la justicia; que lea la inscripción y comprenda mis preciosas palabras: la inscripción le explicará su caso.

#### `09-more.mp3` (dot 9: ¿Qué tan alta es?)

> Este modelo 3D es un escaneo de una copia de yeso del Museo del Antiguo Cercano Oriente de Harvard. La piedra real está en el museo del Louvre, en París, Francia.
